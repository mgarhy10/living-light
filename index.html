How it works

Bioluminescence is a chemical reaction. An organism oxidizes a light-emitting molecule, and the energy released comes out as a photon instead of heat. That's why it's often called "cold light."

Luciferin+ O₂— luciferase → Oxyluciferin+ Light ✦

The fuel: luciferin

"Luciferin" isn't one molecule. It's a family name for whatever compound an organism burns for light. Fireflies, dinoflagellates and many deep-sea animals each use chemically different luciferins.

The spark: luciferase

An enzyme called luciferase speeds the reaction along. Firefly luciferase is so efficient that nearly every reacting molecule produces a photon, which makes it one of the most efficient light sources known.

The color

Most marine light is blue-green, around 470–490 nm, because those wavelengths travel farthest through seawater. On land, yellows and greens are more common.

Who glows

Bioluminescence has evolved independently at least 90 times across the tree of life, in bacteria, fungi, insects, fish, jellyfish and more.

Fireflies

Family Lampyridae

Each species flashes its own pattern, a kind of Morse code that lets males and females find the right partner in the dark.

Dinoflagellates

e.g. Noctiluca scintillans

Single-celled plankton that flash blue when the water around them is disturbed. They're behind the famous "glowing waves."

Anglerfish

Order Lophiiformes

A fleshy lure dangles above the mouth, lit by symbiotic bacteria. Curious prey swim toward the light and get eaten.

Crystal jelly

Aequorea victoria

The source of green fluorescent protein (GFP). Its discovery transformed cell biology and earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Ghost mushrooms

e.g. Omphalotus nidiformis

About a hundred fungi species glow a soft green all night, possibly to attract insects that spread their spores.

Hawaiian bobtail squid

Euprymna scolopes

Glowing bacteria in a special organ match the moonlight from above, erasing the squid's shadow so predators below can't see it.

Why glow?

In the deep ocean, where sunlight never reaches, light is a language. Organisms use it to find food, find mates and avoid becoming food.

Lure

Attract prey with a light they can't resist, like the anglerfish's glowing bait.

Camouflage

"Counter-illumination" uses belly lights to match the glow from above and hide a silhouette.

Burglar alarm

When attacked, some plankton flash to draw in bigger predators that might eat the attacker.

Courtship

Fireflies, ostracods and some fish use precise light signals to find mates.

Where to see it

Go on a dark, moonless night, give your eyes 20 minutes to adjust, and leave the flashlight off.

  1. Mosquito Bay, Vieques, Puerto Rico: often called the brightest bioluminescent bay in the world.
  2. Great Smoky Mountains, USA: synchronous fireflies flash in unison for a few weeks each June.
  3. Waitomo Caves, New Zealand: thousands of glowworms light cave ceilings like a starfield.
  4. Toyama Bay, Japan: firefly squid rise to the surface each spring and light up the shoreline.
  5. Maldives & Southern California coasts: plankton blooms can turn breaking waves electric blue.
